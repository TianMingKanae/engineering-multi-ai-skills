# Paragraphs, sentences and words

A paragraph develops one main point. State it early when that helps the reader, then support it with evidence, an explanation, a definition, a condition or an implication. Split at a change in purpose rather than at an arbitrary word count.

Synthetic example, assuming the measurements actually support it:

> Batching reduced processing time under the tested workload. The batched version performed fewer setup operations, while the input size and validation checks remained unchanged.

Repeat a technical term when it names the same quantity. Do not replace "response time" with "throughput" for variety. Use a transition to expose a real relationship, not to manufacture causality. Replace an ambiguous "This improves accuracy" with a named subject when several possible antecedents exist.

Prefer explicit actors and verbs when they matter: "The checker rejected the record" is clearer than "A rejection of the record was performed." Passive voice remains useful when the actor is unknown, already obvious or irrelevant. Preserve the agent if it affects reproducibility or responsibility.

Make dependencies explicit: use a condition, contrast or cause clause only when the evidence supports that relationship. Put modifiers next to what they modify. An opening phrase must describe the sentence's subject: "After validating the file, the tool imports the records" avoids assigning the validation to the records.

Keep lists and comparisons parallel. Compare a quantity with the corresponding quantity under the alternative, not with the alternative's name alone. Use a restrictive clause to identify which item; a nonrestrictive clause adds information. Follow the target English variety rather than treating every grammatical preference as a universal rule.

Plain wording often helps: "use" instead of "utilize," "because" instead of "due to the fact that," and "before" instead of "prior to." Check before replacing a specialist term with a near-synonym. "Estimate," "measure," "prove" and "verify" make different claims.

Remove filler and duplicated meaning when nothing is lost. Replace vague praise with a sourced effect and condition. Explain practical importance; reserve a claim of statistical significance for the specified analysis. Avoid idioms, euphemisms and demographic stereotypes. A readability score or style flag cannot determine technical accuracy.
