# Paper and report structure

Follow the destination's required format. Use headings that identify the actual question or content; a generic heading may be conventional, while its subsections can be more specific. Avoid long noun strings. Contribution lists should use parallel items and match what the paper establishes.

## Abstract and introduction

An informative abstract gives the problem, approach, main supported finding and important condition or limitation. Use actual values only when sourced. Do not convert a planned experiment into a reported result. Define necessary acronyms independently of the body if the publication expects a standalone abstract.

The introduction supplies enough context to identify the gap, states the purpose and scope, explains the contributions and introduces essential terms. Compare with cited prior work fairly; novelty is a supported distinction, not the adjective "novel". Add a roadmap only when it helps or the venue requires it.

## Methods, results and conclusions

Methods make the observation interpretable and reproducible: inputs, assumptions, comparison groups, workload, settings, stopping rules, evaluation measures, uncertainty procedures and departures from the plan. Give enough detail to judge comparison fairness. Cross-reference supplementary material for lengthy supporting detail without hiding essential conditions.

Results describe observations, including missing/failed cases relevant to the registered inventory. Interpretation explains what those observations support and what remains uncertain. Recommendations or future work specify a next action. Keep these functions distinguishable even if the venue combines sections.

The conclusion answers the stated purpose using established results. It may state limitations and justified next steps; it should not introduce an unsupported result or broaden a conditional finding into a universal claim. A negative or inconclusive finding can be the correct conclusion.

## Reports and supporting material

For a longer report, an executive summary should let a decision-maker understand the problem, finding, consequence, uncertainty and requested action. It differs from a specialist abstract. Use the report's required front matter; do not impose a report title page or contents list on a short article.

Keep the main argument and qualifications in the body. Put extended derivations, full inventories and additional reproducibility detail in clearly referenced appendices or supplements. A reader should be able to locate the evidence supporting each headline statement.
