# Figures, tables and layout

Choose a graphic for the reader's question. A table helps retrieve exact values; a line plot shows change over an ordered variable; bars compare categories; paired points show matched observations; a diagram explains structure or process. Do not imply continuity for unordered categories. Talks usually need less visual detail than manuscripts.

Identify the data, transformations, units and uncertainty represented. State sample size and relevant comparison conditions. Do not omit unfavorable groups or failures just because they complicate the picture. Disclose exclusions and selection rules that affect the interpretation.

Bar lengths normally require a zero baseline. Line and scatter plots can use a nonzero range when appropriate, with clear limits and disclosure of breaks or unusual scales. Label logarithmic axes explicitly. Aspect ratio and cropping can exaggerate a trend; choose them for truthful interpretation, not a predetermined visual effect.

Make captions usable outside the surrounding paragraph: what is compared, under which conditions, what marks/intervals mean and any material limitation. Label axes and units, define legend entries and cite externally sourced data or graphics. The caption's conclusion must match the plotted evidence.

Refer to the graphic in the text and explain the observation the reader should examine. Avoid copying every table entry into prose. Test labels at the final column/page size, with readable contrast and distinguishable marks in grayscale. Do not rely solely on color to identify groups.

Preserve the publisher's or institution's template, including column layout, fonts and spacing. General report-design suggestions do not authorize changes to a manuscript template. Inspect a rendered result after layout changes; source text alone does not establish legibility or correct placement.
