# Articles, tense and multilingual readers

Check articles by noun phrase. A singular countable noun normally needs a determiner: "a parser," "the parser," or "this parser." Use "the" when the item is identified; use no article for suitable general plural or mass nouns. Choose a/an by sound, not spelling: "an hour," "a user."

Use tense for the intended time and claim. Present tense often describes the paper, a figure or an established relationship. Past tense describes a particular completed experiment. Present perfect can describe a body of work continuing to the present. Follow the publication's citation convention consistently; do not change a planned test into a completed one by revising its tense.

Match subjects and verbs across intervening phrases. "The number of records is fixed" differs from "A number of records are missing." In their usual senses, information, equipment and feedback are mass nouns; context still matters for specialist usage.

Keep adverbs next to the words they modify. "Only the first group was tested" differs from "The first group was only tested." Embedded questions use statement order: "We examine whether the limit holds." Keep hypothetical and observed conditions distinguishable.

Prefer established phrasing such as "depend on" and "different from" when appropriate. Check unfamiliar idioms against a suitable dictionary, house style or verified usage; an author's presumed native language is not evidence of correctness.

For readers using English as an additional language, name the subject, use consistent terminology, minimize unexplained abbreviations and avoid culturally specific metaphors. Write dates and units unambiguously. Shorter sentences help when they preserve the logical dependencies; splitting a qualification away from its claim can make the writing less accurate.
